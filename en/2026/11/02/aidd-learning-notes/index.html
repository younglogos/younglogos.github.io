Reflections on Learning AIDD

From drug discovery to CMC: what AIDD can solve, the role algorithms play, and why data ultimately decides success.

Published Mon, Nov 2, 2026 by Junqi Yang

Estimated reading time: 4 min

I have recently been learning about AIDD (AI-assisted drug design). To summarize the field in one sentence: it uses AI models to predict the utility of drug molecules. That utility can be binding to a target, changes in biomarkers after the drug is administered, or even improvement in the disease itself. The drug molecule can be a known molecule or one generated by an AI model. The essence of AI-assisted drug design is to find a functional relationship that describes the mapping between drug molecules and their utility, and then use that relationship to find better drug molecules.

If this were the era before agents existed, my focus would have been on the AI algorithms themselves. But now that agents are here, the algorithm is no longer the hardest part for me — what matters more is clarifying which problems are important and need to be solved. So in AIDD, what is important is not the AI, but the DD. AIDD can be split into two parts: generating molecules, and predicting their utility. Molecule generation tasks often also require predicting utility, so generation and prediction can be integrated into a single model, evaluating utility at the same time as generating.

In the drug discovery stage, the evaluation of utility can be a multi-parameter set including molecular properties (pKa, solubility, thermal stability, and so on), binding affinity to the target, enzyme activity, or the downstream functions the drug molecule participates in. At this stage, the quality and magnitude of a drug’s utility are the primary screening metrics. But whether a molecule can become a PCC also depends critically on its accessibility, metabolism, and toxicity in the in vivo environment. Accessibility refers to whether the drug can effectively enter the target cells; given the enormous difference between in vivo and in vitro environments, this step is difficult to simulate in in vitro experiments. Metabolism is the pharmacological part — a drug goes through uptake, absorption, action, metabolism, and degradation or excretion in the body. Not understanding where a drug goes in the body is unacceptable, whether from a researcher’s perspective or from regulatory and patient perspectives. Toxicity concerns whether taking the drug will cause serious side effects; this step carries more detailed requirements in the toxicology studies required for clinical filing, and needs to be tested during dose escalation. Much of the published research using AI to predict drug utility sits in the drug discovery stage, using the multi-parameter sets mentioned above as prediction targets and molecular structure as input variables, with training data mostly coming from in vitro experiments. For a drug molecule to enter clinical trials, it must first go through non-clinical efficacy and toxicology evaluation.

Beyond drug R&D, a drug’s CMC (Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls) — that is, process optimization and stability — can also be assisted by AI. Once historical process parameters have been trained into a model, the functional relationship between process parameters and a stable product can be found. So every preclinical evaluation step can be supported by AI-assisted prediction. From my own professional experience, what is needed may not be particularly sophisticated algorithms, but high-quality data. There are also many steps in clinical trials where the trial design could be optimized and decisions supported, but since I have never been through one end to end, it is hard for me to offer any useful insights.

Having covered what AIDD can solve, let me return to the algorithms and models. Drug molecules come in many modalities — small molecules, antibodies, peptides, oligonucleotides, and so on — but whichever they are, the chemical essence of a drug molecule is a molecule. Following the plain first principle that structure determines function, a drug molecule’s conformation, functional groups, and three-dimensional coordinates (some use atomic-scale positional coordinates) are the basic information used to characterize a drug. In AIDD, molecules can be generated, or the properties of unknown molecules can be predicted. The mainstream algorithms today are graph neural networks (GNNs) and Transformers. GNNs learn the relationships between neighboring parts within a molecule; Transformers treat the atoms and functional groups on a molecule as “context” in order to predict the entire chemical formula, structure, or properties. For generating molecules, diffusion models are widely used: starting from random noise data and progressively denoising — conditioned denoising — to generate molecules and protein structures. The basic requirement for a generated molecule is that it must bear some similarity to existing drug molecules, that is, possess basic medicinal chemistry properties. Protein drugs need to have normal protein structure — at least resembling an antibody, or a membrane protein, and so on. To help the initially generated molecules meet these basic conditions, pretraining can help. In practice, several algorithms can be combined into a single model. Once I have worked on more real projects, I will study the underlying algorithmic principles in greater detail.

My overall conclusion is that what matters most in AIDD is data, data, data. And the data in the pharmaceutical industry is mostly private, and its quality is uneven — these unsolved problems are exactly where the opportunities lie.
