title = "Lessons from Two Novartis Oligonucleotide Failures"
description = "Reflections on the boundary between mechanistic efficacy and clinical efficacy, drawn from two Novartis oligonucleotide Phase III failures."
date = 2026-10-13T18:00:00+08:00
+++

Novartis' two recent Phase III failures of oligonucleotide drugs have broken my preconception about this class of therapeutics — that suppressing target expression will, with high probability, translate into clinical efficacy. This view is essentially a static worldview: it assumes that flipping a single switch is enough to reverse a disease outcome. In reality, however, outcomes are shaped dynamically. There may be an initial trigger or a dominant factor, but as the disease progresses, multiple forces and effects compete and interact with one another.

Novartis reported two failed pipelines: pelacarsen and del-desiran, targeting cardiovascular disease and type 1 myotonic dystrophy (DM1), respectively. Pelacarsen is an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) that targets the LPA gene, suppressing Lp(a) synthesis at its source. Del-desiran is an antibody–oligonucleotide conjugate (AOC) targeting the DMPK gene.

The target biology for both pipelines is well established. LPA was identified through human genetics, and Mendelian randomization has confirmed a causal relationship between LPA and cardiovascular disease — in theory, lowering LPA expression should benefit cardiovascular outcomes. Pelacarsen had already demonstrated in Phase II that it could knock down Lp(a) expression by 72–80%, validating its pharmacodynamic effect at the biomarker level. Yet in the large Phase III trial, all four clinical endpoints (1, cardiovascular death; 2, non-fatal myocardial infarction; 3, non-fatal stroke; 4, urgent coronary revascularization requiring hospitalization) failed to be met. The DMPK mutation was first identified in DM1 patients; genetic and biochemical studies showed that the CUG repeats in DMPK1 prevent the RNA splicing factor MBNL1 from being properly released. Del-desiran's primary clinical endpoint — video-recorded hand-opening time — also did not meet its goal.

In terms of the underlying biology, both LPA and DMPK1 are highly disease-associated targets, but that does not necessarily mean intervening on them will be effective at the disease level. First, the effects of these genetic mutations begin at birth, and intervening in adulthood may not be enough to reverse damage that has been accumulating for decades. In chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease, multiple health indicators shift over time. Lp(a) may have a large influence early on, but as the disease progresses, other pathological processes become dominant — and some changes (e.g., atherosclerosis, plaque formation, inflammatory remodeling) become irreversible. DM1 faces a similar issue: the ideal causal chain is splicing correction → normalization of protein isoforms → muscle fiber repair / remodeling → functional improvement, but reversing the upstream cause does not necessarily translate into functional benefit if the patient's accumulated fibrosis and fatty replacement are already irreversible. Second, the trial population and the population that informed the target hypothesis are not perfectly matched. For example, pelacarsen enrolled high-risk patients who already had established CVD, whereas the human genetic evidence for low Lp(a) comes from people who simply do not develop the disease at a young age.

Some other analysts and researchers have argued that switching pelacarsen to an siRNA — for higher gene-silencing efficiency — could have improved clinical efficacy. Although one can technically silence the target more thoroughly, pelacarsen was already achieving 70–80% knockdown. I doubt that a further 10–15% improvement in silencing efficiency would yield meaningful marginal clinical benefit. For DMPK, the CUG-repeat RNA is mainly located in the nucleus, so there is also the argument that swapping del-desiran for an ASO with better nuclear entry might have led to a different outcome. For a disease like DM1, however, where the clinical endpoint is gated by irreversible fibrosis and fatty replacement, even the highest possible CUG clearance may not be able to rescue the endpoint.

A strong correlation, even causation, between a biomarker and a disease is the foundation of mechanistic plausibility. The ability of a biotherapeutic to effectively modulate that biomarker represents the molecule's basic capacity to control the disease. But this chain of logic does not always hold up against the complexity of real disease dynamics. To know which targets, at which stages of disease progression, will produce the desired clinical effect requires a sufficiently dynamic understanding of disease development — and that understanding must be system-level and grounded in the real human body. This also carries a lesson for project initiation: it forces us to ask which targets, in the real human environment, exert the strongest influence on disease trajectory.
